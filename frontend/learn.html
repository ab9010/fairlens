The vocabulary of AI ethics, in plain language

Short, student-friendly explanations of the concepts behind FairLens — what bias is, where it comes from, and what it means to build AI responsibly.

AI Ethics

AI ethics is the set of principles that guide how automated systems should be designed and used so they treat people fairly, respect their privacy, and stay accountable to the humans affected by their decisions.

AI Bias

AI bias is a systematic tendency for a model to produce different outcomes for different groups of people, in a way that isn't explained by legitimate differences in the data — often reflecting patterns baked in during data collection, labeling, or training.

Why AI bias matters

Small statistical gaps become real consequences

A 10-point gap in a loan approval rate might look like a rounding error on a spreadsheet, but multiplied across thousands of applicants it means real people denied credit, jobs, or care for reasons that have nothing to do with their qualifications. Because automated systems operate at scale, an unexamined bias doesn't stay small — it gets applied consistently to everyone who passes through the system.

Where bias comes from

Four common sources of bias

Dataset bias

The training data itself doesn't represent the population a system will be used on — for example, a medical model trained mostly on one demographic.

Historical bias

Even a perfectly collected dataset can encode bias if it reflects decisions made under discriminatory past conditions, like historical lending or hiring patterns.

Representation bias

Some groups are proportionally under- or over-represented in the data, which makes the model less reliable — or entirely blind — for underrepresented groups.

Measurement bias

The features or labels used as a stand-in for what you actually care about measure different things across groups, for example using zip code as a rough stand-in for risk.

Algorithmic bias

Bias that's introduced or amplified by the model or algorithm itself — through how it weighs features, optimizes for accuracy, or sets decision thresholds — even when the input data looks reasonable at first glance.

Responsible AI

Six principles that guide how FairLens thinks about fairness

Fairness

Outcomes shouldn't systematically favor or disadvantage a group without a legitimate, disclosed reason.

Transparency

How a system reaches a decision should be understandable and open to inspection, not a black box.

Accountability

Someone is responsible for a system's outcomes and has a process for correcting problems when they're found.

Privacy

Personal data should be collected, stored, and used with the consent and protection people expect.

Safety

A system should be tested for how it fails, not just how it performs when everything goes right.

Explainability

The people affected by a decision should be able to get a clear, honest reason for it.

Fairness score formula

How the 0–100 score is calculated

FairLens starts every dataset at 100 points, then subtracts a penalty for the selection rate gap between groups (up to 60 points) and a penalty for how far the disparate impact ratio falls below the common 0.80 "four-fifths" reference line (up to 40 points). The formula is intentionally simple and disclosed in full so it's easy to audit.

This is an educational composite score, not an official scientific, legal, or regulatory standard.
