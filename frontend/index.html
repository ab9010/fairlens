The problem

Models learn from history, including its mistakes

AI bias happens when a system's outputs systematically favor or disadvantage a group of people, usually because the data it learned from reflects existing social, economic, or measurement imbalances. A hiring model trained on ten years of resumes will happily repeat ten years of hiring patterns — good and bad alike.

Bias isn't always the result of bad intent. It can enter through who was sampled, how an outcome was labeled, which features were available, or how a threshold was set. FairLens is built to surface these gaps quantitatively, so a conversation about fairness can start from evidence rather than assumption.

Why fairness matters

When an automated decision affects someone's access to money, work, care, or education, an unexamined disparity stops being a modeling detail and becomes a real consequence in someone's life. Measuring fairness is how that risk gets caught before deployment rather than after.

Fairness metrics

The measurements behind the score

metric

Selection rate difference

The gap, in percentage points, between the group with the highest positive-outcome rate and the group with the lowest.

metric

Disparate impact

The ratio of the lowest group's selection rate to the highest. A common reference line, the "four-fifths rule," flags ratios under 0.80.

metric

Demographic parity

How close outcome rates are across groups, expressed as 1 minus the selection rate difference — 1.0 means perfect parity.

metric

Equal opportunity

Compares true positive rates across groups when a ground-truth label is available, so qualified people are caught at similar rates.

metric

Group-wise breakdown

Row counts, positive/negative outcome counts, and selection rate for every group in the protected attribute.

metric

Fairness score

A transparent 0–100 composite of the metrics above, with a documented formula — see the Learn page for the full breakdown.
